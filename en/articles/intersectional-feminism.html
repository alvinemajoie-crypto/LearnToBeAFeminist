Intersectional feminism, explained

Understanding 5 October 2026

Not all women experience inequality in the same way. Intersectionality helps explain why.

Where does the concept come from?

In 1989, the American legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw proposed the word “intersectionality” to describe something laws and institutions struggled to see: a person can face several forms of discrimination at once, and their combination creates an experience that is not simply the sum of each.

She started from the situation of Black women in the United States, whose complaints were not recognised when examined only through the lens of sex, or only through the lens of race.

A simple image: the crossroads

Crenshaw uses the image of a crossroads. Several roads meet there: gender, origin, social class, age, disability. A person standing at the crossing can be hit by traffic coming from several directions at the same time.

You cannot effectively fight one inequality while ignoring the others layered on top of it.

What this changes for African women

Gender combines with other factors: living in a city or in the countryside, income level, age, disability, level of education, religion, marital status.

Two girls in the same country do not face the same obstacles to staying in school if one lives near a school and the other must travel long distances, or if one family can pay the fees and the other cannot.

African feminisms

The word was born in the United States, but African thinkers have built their own frameworks. The Nigerian scholar Molara Ogundipe-Leslie, for example, proposed “STIWA” (Social Transformation Including Women in Africa), which links women’s place to the social transformation of the continent.

Why it is useful

  • Seeing which women are overlooked by policies or programmes.
  • Designing actions suited to local realities rather than a single model.
  • Listening to those directly concerned before speaking on their behalf.
